Worked examples

Uniform 4-bay NAS: 4 × 8 TB (32 TB raw / 29.10 TiB)

With four matching 8 TB drives, there is no mixed-size waste, so single-parity layouts all match on raw math: RAID 5, Synology SHR and ZFS RAIDZ1 give 24 TB (21.83 TiB) usable at 75% efficiency and survive 1 drive failure. Dual-parity and mirrored layouts (RAID 6, RAID 10, SHR-2, ZFS 2-way mirrors and RAIDZ2) give 16 TB (14.55 TiB) usable at 50% efficiency. RAID 1 keeps four full copies of the same 8 TB (7.28 TiB) and survives up to 3 failures. Subtract roughly 5% for ext4/Btrfs overhead (~20.74 TiB on RAID 5/SHR) or roughly 8% for ZFS slop and metadata (~20.08 TiB on RAIDZ1).

Mixed 4-bay NAS: 2 × 4 TB + 2 × 8 TB (24 TB raw / 21.83 TiB)

This is where mixed-drive handling changes the math. Standard RAID 5 and ZFS RAIDZ1 clamp every drive to the smallest disk (4 TB), leaving 4 TB unused on each 8 TB drive (8 TB wasted) and yielding 12 TB (10.91 TiB) usable. Synology SHR slices the drives into a 4 × 4 TB RAID 5 layer (12 TB usable, 4 TB parity) plus a 2 × 4 TB RAID 1 mirror on the remaining halves of the 8 TB drives (4 TB usable, 4 TB mirror), giving 16 TB (14.55 TiB) usable with zero wasted space. Synology SHR-2 still yields 8 TB (7.28 TiB) here because a 2-drive-redundant RAID 6 layer needs at least four drives of the larger size. ZFS 2-way mirrors can pair matching disks into two separate mirror vdevs (4 TB + 4 TB and 8 TB + 8 TB) for 12 TB (10.91 TiB) usable and zero wasted space.

How the math works

Given n drives sorted from smallest to largest (d1 ≤ … ≤ dn) with total raw capacity R = ∑di and smallest drive s = d1:

  • RAID 1 (mirror, 2+ drives): Usable = s, survives n − 1 failures. Every disk holds a full copy of the smallest drive.
  • RAID 5 (3+ drives) and ZFS RAIDZ1 (3+ drives): Usable = (n − 1) × s, survives 1 drive failure.
  • RAID 6 (4+ drives) and ZFS RAIDZ2 (4+ drives): Usable = (n − 2) × s, survives 2 drive failures.
  • ZFS RAIDZ3 (5+ drives): Usable = (n − 3) × s, survives 3 drive failures.
  • RAID 10 (4+ even drives): Striped 2-way mirrors clamped to s: usable = (n / 2) × s. Survives 1 drive guaranteed, or up to n / 2 if only one drive dies per pair.
  • ZFS 2-way mirrors (2+ even drives): Pairs adjacent sorted drives into separate mirror vdevs, so each pair contributes its smaller drive: usable = d1 + d3 + … + dn−1. Survives 1 drive per mirror vdev.
  • Synology SHR (2+ drives): Slices the array into equal-height horizontal layers across all drives tall enough to participate (Synology SHR documentation, read November 26, 2026). Every layer with 2 or more drives forms a RAID 1 or RAID 5 stripe; only the excess of the single largest drive above the second-largest drive is unused. That simplifies to usable = R − dn and survives 1 drive failure.
  • Synology SHR-2 (4+ drives): Uses the same slicing rule with 2-drive parity, so each layer needs at least 4 drives to form a RAID 6 stripe. Excess capacity on the top three drives above the fourth-largest drive stays unused until four drives reach that size. Survives 2 drive failures.

What is approximate

  • Decimal TB vs binary TiB: Drive labels use base-10 terabytes (1 TB = 1012 bytes), while Linux, TrueNAS, DSM and Windows often show base-2 tebibytes (1 TiB = 240 bytes). Converting TB to TiB multiplies by 10^12 / 2^40 (≈ 0.9095), a 9.05% drop before any parity or filesystem formatting.
  • Filesystem overhead: Traditional filesystems reserve space for metadata, journals and root blocks (ext4 reserves 5% for root by default on un-tuned volumes), and NAS operating systems like Synology DSM keep a small system partition on every drive. The breakdown's post-overhead estimate subtracts a rough 5% for standard RAID and SHR layouts; your actual formatted free space depends on filesystem and mount options.
  • ZFS slop space and RAIDZ padding: OpenZFS reserves 1 / 2^spa_slop_shift of pool capacity so metadata writes cannot wedge a full pool; the default spa_slop_shift=5 reserves 1/32, or 3.125% (rounded to 3.2% in the OpenZFS module parameters documentation, read November 26, 2026). On top of slop space, ZFS uses space for metadata and RAIDZ stripe padding (which varies with ashift, recordsize and vdev width), and many operators keep ZFS pools under 80% full to avoid fragmentation slowdowns. The breakdown subtracts a rough 8% for ZFS as a planning estimate, not an exact allocation simulation.
  • Single-vdev RAIDZ: The RAIDZ rows model a single vdev of all entered drives. Wider pools (often past 8 to 12 drives) are usually split into multiple vdevs, which costs one parity drive per extra vdev.
RAID is not a backup. Parity and mirrors keep your NAS running when a disk dies, but an accidental delete, ransomware, filesystem bug, power surge or fire wipes every copy in the box at once. Pair your array with scheduled, encrypted off-site backups.

Questions

Why does an 8 TB drive show as 7.28 TiB?

Drive makers sell capacity in decimal terabytes (1 TB = 1,000,000,000,000 bytes), while Linux, TrueNAS and Windows report binary tebibytes (1 TiB = 1,099,511,627,776 bytes). Dividing 8 by 1.099511627776 gives 7.28 TiB before any parity or filesystem formatting.

How does Synology SHR use mixed drive sizes when RAID 5 does not?

Standard RAID 5 clamps every drive to the smallest disk in the array, leaving extra capacity on larger drives unused. Synology SHR slices drives into equal-sized layers and builds a RAID 5 or RAID 1 array across each layer with two or more drives, so 2 × 4 TB plus 2 × 8 TB gives 16 TB usable instead of 12 TB.

Does ZFS RAIDZ1 or RAIDZ2 support mixed drive sizes?

You can build a RAIDZ vdev with mixed drive sizes, but ZFS treats every drive in the vdev as the size of the smallest drive until all drives are replaced with larger ones. If you have matching pairs of different sizes, such as two 4 TB drives and two 8 TB drives, separate ZFS 2-way mirror vdevs use all four drives with no wasted space.

Is RAID 5 or ZFS RAIDZ enough without a backup?

No. RAID, SHR and ZFS protect uptime when a drive fails, but accidental deletions, ransomware, bad migrations, power surges and fire hit the whole array at once. Keep an off-site versioned backup alongside your NAS.
